## Research Brief

**Core insight:** Battery duration (4-hour vs. 8-hour) determines which grid services a storage asset can profitably provide. It's not about "more storage is better"—it's about matching duration to the market's actual needs.

**Key facts:**
- 4-hour batteries excel at energy arbitrage (charge at noon, discharge at evening peak). Typical daily cycle.
- 8-hour batteries capture multi-peak events (charge at noon, discharge 5 PM–1 AM), useful for regions with evening AND late-night peaks
- 4-hour projects can perform frequency regulation (fast response services) AND energy arbitrage; 8-hour units are locked into energy-only contracts most days
- Revenue stacking: 4-hour systems earn $15–25/kW-year from ancillary services; 8-hour systems earn $5–10/kW-year (limited duration = fewer opportunities)
- Capex: 8-hour systems cost 15–20% more per kW (more battery pack) but don't generate proportional revenue uplift in markets with 4-hour focus

**Angle:** Developers choose duration based on local grid profile, not technology advancement. Mismatch = stranded asset economics.

## Post Draft (EDITING COMPLETE)

```
"We're planning a battery storage project. Should we go 4-hour or 8-hour duration?"

The wrong answer is "as much as possible." Duration isn't a "more is better" game.

A 4-hour battery system is designed for a single, sharp peak. Charge mid-morning through early 
afternoon (low solar cost or low demand pricing). Discharge during the evening peak (5–9 PM, 
highest prices). Repeat daily. This system is fast and capital-efficient. It can also provide 
frequency regulation and voltage support because it cycles every day and has available dispatch windows.

An 8-hour system stretches the discharge window. It targets regions with both an evening peak (5 PM) 
and a late-night peak (8–11 PM). It charges once and discharges over a longer window. This works 
in markets like California and parts of Texas where demand stays elevated until late evening.

But here's where duration economics diverge:

In a 4-hour market (most of North America), an 8-hour system is oversized. You pay for 8 hours of 
storage but only use 4 hours of value every day. The extra 4 hours of battery capacity sits idle, 
generating no revenue. Meanwhile, ancillary services (frequency regulation, demand response)—which 
pay $15–25/kW-year—require frequent dispatch. An 8-hour system locked into energy arbitrage can't 
cycle fast enough to capture those services.

Capex: an 8-hour system costs 15–20% more per kW than a 4-hour system. Revenue gap: 8-hour systems 
earn roughly half the ancillary-service revenue because they're committed to energy-only contracts.

The math: choose 8-hour duration only if your market has a documented multi-peak profile that 
justifies the extra capex. Otherwise, a 4-hour system, with revenue stacking from ancillary 
services, outperforms.

Storage isn't one-size-fits-all. Duration is a market read, not an engineering choice.
```
